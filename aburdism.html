Philosophy

Albert Camus is generally considered the main figure behind absurdism, although the philosophy was influenced by earlier thinkers. Camus developed the idea in the 20th century, especially in The Myth of Sisyphus, where he argued that humans naturally search for meaning and purpose while the universe does not provide clear answers. He called this conflict “the absurd.” Rather than believing life is meaningless and giving up, Camus argued that people should accept this reality and continue living fully, creating experiences and embracing life despite uncertainty. His work helped establish absurdism as an important philosophical perspective.

Albert Camus Books

The Rebel (L'Homme révolté)

A foundational text in absurdism that explores how to live ethically without overarching meaning.

The Myth of Sisyphus

A 1942 philosophical essay by Albert Camus that introduces his philosophy of the absurd, exploring the conflict between humanity's search for meaning and the universe's silent indifference.

The Stranger

Follows Meursault, a detached and indifferent man, whose life unravels after he commits a seemingly senseless murder. It is widely considered the best starting point for beginners.

The Plague

An allegorical exploration of the absurd. As a devastating plague hits an Algerian city, it forces the characters to grapple with the inherent meaninglessness of the disaster and fight against it through solidarity and compassion.
